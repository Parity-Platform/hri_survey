# HRIT Synthesis Report

Stakeholder perceptions of collaborative robotics in small manufacturing SMEs

Parity Platform P.C. and University of Patras, 2026. Licence: Apache 2.0.

## 1. Purpose and scope

The report summarises how manual workers, operators, supervisors, SME clients and members of the public perceive Human-Robot Interaction (HRI) in small manufacturing environments. It documents perceived benefits, risks and ethical considerations, and the transparency and engagement principles co-created by participants. It does not recommend for or against adoption.

## 2. Method

| Element | Implementation |
|---|---|
| Case study sites | 5 manufacturing SMEs in Greece, including PHEE and Meazon in Patras |
| Participant register | 80+ contacts, balanced by gender, age and professional role |
| Interviews | 30 semi-structured interviews (guide in [04_interview_guide.md](04_interview_guide.md)) |
| Surveys | 50+ validated responses across four role-based questionnaires: Manual Worker, Operator, Supervisor, Future Impact |
| Workshops | 3 participatory workshops: 1 on-site at a Patras SME, 2 at the University of Patras; 45+ participants in total |
| Period | Data collection November 2025 to February 2026; workshops February to April 2026; synthesis May to June 2026 |
| Ethics and data | Informed consent, anonymisation before analysis, GDPR-compliant processing ([08_data_and_anonymisation.md](08_data_and_anonymisation.md)) |

Survey data were analysed descriptively by stakeholder category, role and prior exposure to robots. Interview and workshop transcripts were coded thematically by two researchers, one from each partner, and discrepancies were resolved by discussion. Workshop participants reviewed the draft findings and principles before publication.

## 3. Findings

### 3.1 Acceptance is conditional

Sentiment towards collaborative robots is cautiously positive. Workers and supervisors recognise gains in productivity, physical strain and safety. Support depends on three conditions stated across all groups: transparent communication about the purpose of the robot, hands-on training before deployment, and a real voice in the decision.

### 3.2 Training is the most consistent request

Training was the single most frequent request across all audiences. Manual workers and operators reported that the main barrier is not the robot itself, but the lack of practical training and of clear procedures when something goes wrong on the line. Short theoretical briefings were considered insufficient.

### 3.3 Trust is built or lost at supervisor level

Workers tend to mirror the stance of their supervisor. Where supervisors were prepared and communicated openly, worker concerns were lower. Where supervisors were uncertain or uninformed, rumours filled the gap. This finding led to the [supervisor briefing pack](07_supervisor_briefing_pack.md).

### 3.4 Job displacement fear is task-specific

Fear of job loss was present but narrower than expected. Participants worried about specific tasks being removed rather than about losing their job entirely. The concern dropped markedly when concrete retraining options were presented together with the technology, and rose when automation was discussed without any plan for the people affected.

### 3.5 Perceived benefits and risks

| Perceived benefits | Perceived risks |
|---|---|
| Reduced physical strain on repetitive or heavy tasks | Stress caused by unexpected robot actions |
| Consistent product quality | Mental load of monitoring a robot during shared tasks |
| Fewer injuries from manual handling | Unclear responsibility when the system makes an error |
| Opportunity to learn new technical skills | Loss of tasks and future reduction of staff |
| Relief from labour shortages | Robot output not justifying the investment |

### 3.6 Ethical considerations raised

- **Responsibility for errors.** Operators reported feeling held responsible for mistakes made by the system. Participants asked for written rules on accountability.
- **Control and autonomy.** Workers want to remain in control of the workflow rather than paced by the robot.
- **Use of performance data.** Participants asked that data generated by robots are not used to monitor individual workers without consent.
- **Fair access to new roles.** Older workers and workers with lower formal qualifications asked for equal access to retraining.
- **Honest framing.** Participants objected to automation being presented only as "modernisation" when cost reduction is a driver.

### 3.7 Differences between groups

- Operators who interact with robots daily reported higher confidence and lower perceived risk than workers with little or no exposure.
- Prior exposure to robots was associated with lower perceived risk across all groups.
- Supervisors placed the main readiness gaps in communication, training and cross-department coordination rather than in the technology itself.
- Breakdowns by gender, age and role are available in the dashboards. With 50+ survey responses these comparisons are descriptive and are used to guide inclusive engagement, not to draw statistical conclusions.

## 4. Co-created transparency and engagement principles

The three workshops produced ten principles. They are the core of the HRIT Toolkit and apply to any automation decision that affects people on the production floor.

1. **Consult before you choose.** Engage affected staff before a technology or supplier is selected, while their input can still change the decision.
2. **State the real purpose.** Explain why automation is considered (cost, quality, safety, labour shortage) and which drivers matter most.
3. **Talk about tasks, not only jobs.** Describe which tasks change, which stay and which are new.
4. **Pair technology with training.** Announce the training and retraining plan in the same message as the deployment plan.
5. **Brief supervisors first.** Prepare supervisors before workers, so that they can answer questions consistently.
6. **Define escalation.** Before go-live, publish who to call, how to stop the robot and what happens after an incident.
7. **Clarify accountability.** State in writing who is responsible when a robot or its software makes an error.
8. **Protect worker data.** Do not use robot data to assess individual performance without explicit agreement.
9. **Include every voice.** Balance participation by gender, age, role, seniority and language; track and publish representation.
10. **Close the loop.** Publish the anonymised findings and explain how participant input changed, or did not change, the decision.

## 5. Implications

| Audience | Implication |
|---|---|
| SMEs | Budget for training and supervisor preparation as part of the automation investment, not as an afterthought |
| Worker representatives | Use the questionnaires and principles as a structured basis for consultation |
| Vocational training centres | Develop short, hands-on modules for collaborative robot operation, troubleshooting and safe recovery |
| Policymakers | Link public support for automation in SMEs to evidence of prior staff consultation and a retraining plan |
| Researchers | Reuse the open schema to compare HRI perceptions across sectors and regions |

## 6. Scope of validity

The evidence comes from five manufacturing SMEs in Western Greece and the participants who volunteered. Findings describe perceptions in that context and are presented as transferable practice, supported by the open instruments and schema that allow other organisations to test them in their own setting.
